Architectura: Italy Drawn

Italy, drawn line by line.

An atlas of Italian architecture that teaches you to read a building the way architects draw it: 47 measured plates from Aosta to Sicily, every part named in Latin, Italian and English, the real sun of Rome falling through the Pantheon’s oculus, and seven courses with examinations and diplomas.

Download on theApp StoreEPRIS Museum →

For iPhone and iPad · in English, Russian and Italian. No account, no ads, no tracking. Made by the editors of EPRIS Journal.

Plates

A drawing that inks itself in.

Each building is a measured line drawing in the EPRIS Museum’s style. Open one and it draws itself line by line; pinch to look closer and tap the pins to learn every part.

599 parts
Each with its proper name in Latin or Italian, its English name, the word an Italian guide would use, a short explanation and one fact worth keeping.
The narrator
Says every term in Italian and reads the lesson aloud, so the names sound the way they are said in Italy.
The sun of Rome
In the Pantheon, a disc of light moves across the coffers and the floor in real time, as it does in Rome right now. Slide through the day, or watch the night.
3D models
Fourteen buildings can be turned in 3D and placed in the room in augmented reality.

The atlas

Every region of Italy, on one map.

A map of Italy with every plate on it, from the Arch of Augustus at Aosta to the nuraghe of Barumini, and plans of Rome and Milan where several buildings stand close together.

Tabula Italiae
Tap a city to open its plates; Rome and Milan open their own plans.
Tempora
A timeline of thirty-six centuries, from the nuraghi to the Bosco Verticale; where centuries pass without a plate, the line folds.
One scale
All the buildings side by side at the same scale: the Pantheon next to the Pirelli tower.
Sources
Official names, UNESCO entries and a reading list for every plate, and a printable A3 sheet of each drawing.

Milan in detail

A whole city, drawn and walked.

Milan has its own course and its own walk: from the Roman columns of San Lorenzo to the Bosco Verticale, through the cathedral, the Castello, La Scala and the Galleria.

The cathedral in section
From the floor of the nave up to the golden Madonnina, 108.5 metres above the square.
The Galleria
The Ottagono, the barrel vaults and the dome of Mengoni’s arcade, part by part.
Modern Milan
The Velasca, Pirelli and Bosco Verticale towers, drawn like the monuments around them.
A walk
Mediolanum takes you through the city plate by plate, in the order you would walk it.

Schola

A real course of study.

Seven courses – Fundamenta, Roma Aeterna, Italia, Ingenium, Magistri, Peregrinatio and Mediolanum – in 40 books and 86 lessons. Fundamenta comes first; the others stand on it.

Examinations
Every book ends in an exam with questions drawn afresh each time: whose detail is this, when was it begun, how tall is it, where does it stand.
Rigor
Choose your level, from Tiro to Magister.
Diplomas
Finish a course for a diploma with your name, and sit the great examination on the whole atlas.
Handouts
Every book prints as an A4 handout with its questions and a key for the teacher.

Ludus

Practice that remembers.

Short drills between the lessons, so the names and places stay with you.

Ubi?
Where does it stand? A building is named and you tap its place on the map of Italy.
Probatio
Rounds of five questions on the plates you have studied, with your score as you go.
Reviews
What you are about to forget comes back on time; the schedule is kept on your own devices.
Studium
Your streak and the reviews due today, on the home screen and in widgets.

Aula

A classroom mode for the projector.

Present a plate on a projector or a mirrored screen: the drawing large, one part at a time, lettered to be read from the back of the room.

One part at a time
Step through the parts with the arrows; the rest of the drawing stays quiet.
Ask first
Hide the names and let the class answer before the label appears.
For teachers
Lesson handouts and printable sheets to give out after the class.
Accessible
VoiceOver reads every plate, and questions that need the eye are asked another way.

A closer look

A few screens in detail: what is on them and why.

Hodie: today

The home screen opens on the plate of the day, the hour in Rome and your study streak. Below are the reviews due and where you left off.

The Pantheon at this hour

The oculus is drawn as it is: a round opening 30 Roman feet across, the only window. The disc of sunlight moves with the real time in Rome; drag the slider to follow it through the day.

Siena on parchment

The Palazzo Pubblico and the Torre del Mangia in the Charta paper: sepia and red chalk. The tower is 88 metres of brick, built so it would rise higher than the cathedral’s bell tower.

Thirty-six centuries

Tempora runs from the nuraghe of Barumini to the spire of Turin, in the Caerulea paper: white lines on blueprint blue.

The Ottagono

Where the arms of the Galleria cross, the octagon opens: 36.6 metres between opposite sides, the meeting place of the city.

Velarium

In Aula the Colosseum’s awning is explained to a whole class: a sailcloth roof rigged from masts round the rim, worked by sailors from the fleet.

Three papers

Nox and Charta

Nox draws lines of light on the night. Charta sets the plates in sepia and red chalk on parchment. The app icon changes with the paper you choose.

Caerulea and widgets

Caerulea is white line on blueprint blue. Widgets on the home screen show the part of the day, your reviews due and the sun over Rome.
