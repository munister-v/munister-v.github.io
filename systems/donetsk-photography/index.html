A city you can now only walk through in pictures

Four hundred and thirty-eight photographs of a place that cannot be visited. An archive like this has one obligation above all the others: never to publish an image it cannot account for.

Why the rules are strict

An archive of a city that can no longer be entered carries a burden an ordinary gallery does not. Every claim it makes — this street, this year, this photographer — may be the only surviving record of that claim. A wrong caption here does not annoy a visitor; it enters circulation and becomes what people believe.

So the design rule is that an unaccountable image is not published. Not published with a hedge, not published pending clarification. Held. An archive is trusted because of what it refuses to say.

The pipeline

Photographs arrive from scattered places: personal collections, freely licensed archives, family albums. The same frame arrives repeatedly under different filenames, in different sizes, sometimes re-cropped — so de-duplication cannot work on names. It works on the image itself.

One of the eight halls is not mine at all: it hangs freely licensed photographs by others, credited by name and licence. That hall exists because an honest archive should show where its own collection ends, and because material somebody else released for reuse is exactly what a public museum should be reusing — with attribution attached to the work rather than buried in a colophon.

The unglamorous part

A significant share of the effort went into filenames. Cyrillic characters, transliterated inconsistently across a decade of different people's habits, arriving in several encodings, some of which survive a copy between operating systems and some of which do not. The same photograph might be present three times, spelled three ways, none of which sort next to each other.

This is worth stating plainly because it is the actual work in most archival projects and it never appears in the write-up. The interesting design decisions took an afternoon. Making four hundred inconsistently named files resolve into four hundred distinct photographs took considerably longer.

Letting readers contribute

People have photographs of this city in boxes. Inviting them in is the point of the whole project — and it is also the moment an archive becomes an upload endpoint on the public internet, which is a different and less pleasant kind of system.

Three decisions carry that design, and all three are about limiting what an unknown file can do.

  • A separate service. Intake runs apart from the museum, so a flood of submissions cannot take the archive down with it, and its failures are its own.
  • Re-encoding, not storing. The uploaded bytes are decoded and a fresh image written from the result. This is the single most valuable step: it discards embedded metadata, including location data the sender did not know was there, and anything hidden in the container that hoped to be served back later.
  • Nothing publishes itself. Submissions wait for a curator. A public archive that hangs whatever it is sent is not an archive.
A note on the interface

The subject sets the register

The temptation with material like this is to reach for atmosphere: dramatic transitions, a soundtrack, sepia. It would be easy, and it would be a way of performing feeling on someone else's behalf.

The museum is instead plainly typeset, the photographs are large, and the captions state what is known and stop. One dark section sits under the opening and quotes a line of theory about images replacing the places they depict — which is the argument the whole site is making, and the only place it is made out loud. Restraint here is not a style preference. It leaves room for the reader to bring their own memory of the street.
